Earthquake geotechnical engineering addresses the behavior of soil and rock under seismic loading and the design of geotechnical systems to withstand earthquakes.

## Seismic Hazard and Ground Motion

The starting point for any seismic analysis is the characterization of the expected ground motion at the site. This typically involves:

- Definition of the design earthquake (or spectrum)
- Consideration of local site effects (amplification or deamplification)
- Selection or generation of acceleration time histories

Soft soils can significantly amplify ground motions, especially at longer periods, while rock sites tend to transmit higher-frequency energy.

## Seismic Site Response Analysis

Site response analysis evaluates how seismic waves propagate through the soil profile. Methods range from:

- Simplified code-based site factors
- One-dimensional equivalent-linear analysis (e.g., SHAKE)
- Non-linear time-domain analysis
- Two- and three-dimensional numerical modeling for complex geometries

The output includes surface acceleration, response spectra, and shear strain profiles that inform structural design and liquefaction assessment.

## Soil Liquefaction

Liquefaction is one of the most dramatic and damaging geotechnical phenomena during earthquakes. It occurs in saturated, loose, cohesionless soils when cyclic loading generates high excess pore pressures, reducing effective stress and shear strength nearly to zero.

### Evaluation of Liquefaction Potential

The most common approach is the **simplified procedure** based on the cyclic stress ratio (CSR) and cyclic resistance ratio (CRR):

$$\text{Factor of Safety} = \frac{\text{CRR}}{\text{CSR}}$$

CRR is typically estimated from SPT, CPT, or shear-wave velocity data, corrected for magnitude and overburden stress.

### Consequences of Liquefaction
- Loss of bearing capacity
- Lateral spreading
- Settlement and ground oscillation
- Increased lateral pressures on retaining structures

## Seismic Design of Foundations

Foundations must be designed for:

- Increased vertical and horizontal loads
- Potential reduction in soil strength
- Kinematic interaction (for deep foundations)
- Inertial interaction with the superstructure

Pile foundations are often preferred in liquefiable ground because they can transfer loads to deeper, more competent strata. However, they must be designed to resist lateral spreading forces and potential downdrag.

## Seismic Slope Stability

Pseudostatic analysis applies a horizontal seismic coefficient to a conventional limit-equilibrium analysis. More advanced approaches include:

- Newmark sliding-block analysis (for permanent displacement)
- Dynamic numerical analysis

## Mitigation Measures

Common strategies to mitigate seismic geotechnical hazards include:

- Ground densification
- Drainage improvement
- Reinforcement (stone columns, deep soil mixing)
- Structural measures (deep foundations, base isolation)
- Avoidance of highly susceptible sites when feasible

## Closing Thoughts

Earthquake geotechnical engineering requires integration of seismology, soil dynamics, and conventional geotechnical principles. As seismic design codes continue to evolve, the demand for rigorous site-specific analysis continues to grow.
