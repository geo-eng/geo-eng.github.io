Slope stability analysis determines whether an embankment can maintain adequate resistance against sliding under defined loading conditions. Because pore pressure, shear strength, material zoning, and foundation geometry interact, the analysis should be treated as a geotechnical model rather than a software exercise.

## 1. Select realistic strength parameters

For new fills, laboratory testing should represent the expected compacted state. The selected shear strength should reflect density, water content, stress path, drainage condition, and material variability.

For effective-stress analysis:

$$\tau_f = c' + (\sigma_n-u)\tan\phi'$$

where $c'$ and $\phi'$ are effective cohesion and friction angle, $\sigma_n$ is total normal stress, and $u$ is pore-water pressure.

## 2. Analyze the critical loading cases

Typical cases include:

1. End of construction.
2. Normal reservoir operation.
3. Maximum or high reservoir level.
4. Rapid drawdown where relevant.
5. Seismic loading.
6. Special transient conditions identified by the project risk assessment.

The exact cases and acceptance criteria must come from the governing dam-safety requirements.

## 3. Understand the role of pore pressure

A slope can have adequate geometry but inadequate stability if pore pressures are underestimated. Seepage analyses, piezometric data, and transient reservoir conditions should therefore be linked to the stability model.

## 4. Search for meaningful failure surfaces

Limit-equilibrium methods can evaluate circular and non-circular potential failure surfaces. The analyst should not rely only on the automatically reported minimum factor of safety. Review the location, depth, material boundaries, and physical plausibility of the critical surface.

## 5. Interpret factors of safety correctly

A factor of safety is not a probability of failure. It is a ratio comparing available resistance with the mobilized driving demand under a defined analysis model.

USACE slope-stability guidance specifies design conditions and minimum factors of safety for embankment dams and emphasizes the importance of appropriate shear-strength characterization. [1]

## 6. Use sensitivity analysis

Change key variables such as:

- effective friction angle;
- undrained shear strength;
- pore pressure;
- foundation strength;
- reservoir level;
- unit weight;
- drainage effectiveness.

A stable design should not depend on one optimistic parameter.

## 7. Consider deformation when necessary

Limit equilibrium is not sufficient for every problem. Where large deformation, seismic loading, weak foundations, or complex construction stages govern, finite-element or other numerical deformation analyses may provide additional insight.

## Practical review checklist

Before accepting a stability model, verify:

- geometry matches the latest drawings;
- material zones are correctly assigned;
- groundwater and pore pressures are defensible;
- strength parameters match the drainage condition;
- loading cases are complete;
- critical surfaces are physically credible;
- sensitivity results are documented.
